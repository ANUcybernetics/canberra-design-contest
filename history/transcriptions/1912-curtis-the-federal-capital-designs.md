# Summary

- L. A. Curtis reviews the competition designs for Australia’s Federal Capital at Yass-Canberra in a paper read on 20 August 1912 and published in *The Surveyor* on 31 August 1912.
- He argues that attractive drawings and geometric symmetry received too much weight, while terrain, street gradients, drainage, railway access, and construction and maintenance costs received too little.
- Curtis favours the Australian Design No. 10, selected first in Mr. Coane’s minority report, as a practical and economical plan that also offers a beautiful city.
- He criticises the prize-winning designs, including the American Design No. 29, for engineering difficulties involving steep roads, railway placement, floodwater, and ornamental lakes.
- He reviews numerous other entries and describes his vision of a capital with impressive public buildings, generous streets, parks, gardens, and room for attractive landscaping.
- Chas. H. Caswell, speaking for the authors of Design No. 10, defends their collaborative approach and explains their railway, lake, spillway, stormwater, and sewerage proposals.

# The Federal Capital Designs

*Read at a Meeting of the Institution of Surveyors, N.S.W., by the President, Mr. L. A. Curtis, on Tuesday, August 20th, 1912.*

A LARGE number of designs for the laying out of the Federal Capital at Yass-Canberra were exhibited at the Sydney Town Hall for a few days in July, which afforded those interested in the study of town-planning a good opportunity of judging what the ideas on the subject are in various parts of the world.

Of course an exhaustive study of the plans sent in was not practicable under the conditions in which they were shown. Nevertheless, a good idea of the relative value of some of the best could be arrived at by those whose daily duties impose upon them the consideration of conditions which operate in the laying out of a city.

I do not claim any especial qualification more than other surveyors in forming opinions about these plans, but I do feel that, as President of this Institution, I might be expected to give members the benefit of any opinions which I may have formed, and I do feel that I can speak with some small amount of authority, because of my long association, extending over 36 years, with a profession which, above all others, is qualified to deal with this subject.

As one might expect from a competition open to the whole world, there are some very fine specimens of town-planning amongst them, and some very beautiful drawings into which many long and tedious days, weeks, even months of close labour have been put. But there are also many mediocre, some bad, and one or two actually bizarre productions on show.

Naturally, those which stand out at first glance are the ones in which draftsmanship excels, and they, generally speaking, are the favoured ones among the general public. But it does not by any means follow, as surveyors well know, that a pretty drawing is the best suited to the conditions. On the contrary, it might almost be said that the reverse is true for any country presenting features which have to be dealt with. Plans aiming at slavish symmetry usually go at features as though they did not exist, and will climb over steep hills at grades of 1 in 8 or 1 in 10, where by a little judicious handling, 1 in 20 or even flatter could easily be obtained. Among these designs a good deal of this is observable, and, unfortunately, the competitor who gives these important considerations attention is handicapped against the first-mentioned by his apparent want of artistic effect. The practical is overshadowed by the beautiful on paper, and the best workable design is relegated to the background, while the impracticable one gets the prize.

I am reluctant to say so, but I am afraid that this is what has happened in the allotment of the prizes in the present competition.

The civil engineer and the surveyor, and more especially the engineering surveyor, are the most competent persons to deal with town-planning, because it is pre-eminently their business, and the architect is less so, unless he combines the business of surveyor with it, because he only comes in when the town has been laid out, and he has to begin the building up of the city after the surveyor has completed his work. The architect would, for the same reason, be the better person to select the sites for public buildings, &c. Now the Board appointed to judge these plans was composed of one architect, one engineer (not a civil engineer, but a mechanical one, I understand), and one engineer surveyor. It is significant that the minority report emanated from the latter gentleman, and I believe that any surveyor who likes to go and look closely into the winning designs, both majority and minority, will not have very much difficulty in finding the reasons which actuated Mr. Coane in his judgment. No. 29 is the First Prize design, and No. 10 is the First Minority design, and I have no hesitation whatever in saying that of the two, No. 10 will be an easier city to lay out, will have better graded streets, will be equally as beautiful as the other, and will be much cheaper to prepare in the first instance and to maintain afterwards.

I do not think it probable that any of the three winning designs will ever materialise, because they are more ideal than practical. Mr. Coane’s choice, however, has, on the contrary, been designed to suit the locality and to minimise the engineering works necessary to make the most of the site. The streets have been located with due regard to grading and economy of construction, while the aesthetic has not been neglected.

I will now give a short description of some of the plans. I could not hope to notice them all, but will say a few words with regard to some of those which attract most attention.

No. 29.—The First Prize, £1750. American. Speaking generally, the design is bold and attractive. Commanding positions have been chosen for the Capitol on Kurrajong, a hill 2000 feet above sea level, and Parliament and the Government group on Camphill Hill, 1928 feet, and the saddle between, 1920 feet above sea level. To the east, north, and north-west the Molonglo River winds its way through the alluvial flats, to be converted into lakes, over which a fine view will be obtained of the Barracks on Russell Hill, the Church Reserve, the Public Gardens adjoining the river flats to the north-east, whilst a little to the north of east, and about a mile and a half away, will be seen the City Hall Law Courts, Post Office, and Gaol on Vernon Hill in the midst of what appears to be the main business portion of the city. A little to the west of north will be seen the Hospital, the University, and the Terrace Heights settlement on the slopes beyond.

The Botanical Gardens will occupy both sides of a sigmoid bend in the river, about a mile and a half away to the north-west. The City Hall occupies Vernon Hill, 1900 feet above sea level, and commands extensive views. The Capitol, The City Hall and the Railway Station occupy the three angles of an equilateral triangle, with sides about 9000 feet long, along which run straight avenues, about 200 feet wide, forming noble thoroughfares.

The Railway has been moved from its central position on the contour plans to one about 5000 feet more to the north-east and east, necessitating a tunnel through the Church Hill, and in which tunnel appears the Railway Station.

The Railway and its position are features which I do not like in the design, because it serves the city far less efficiently than in its originally intended location. The Station, being on the extreme east of the town, is a mile and a half from the Capitol, and three miles from the western residential area, while putting the Station in a tunnel scarcely appeals to one as being the best place for it.

Another very bad feature in connection with it is the large number of streets crossing the line along its length. In its passage through the city there are no less than 26 such crossings shown. I am afraid our Railway Commissioners would strongly object, especially if there be level crossings, to such a state of things in any Railway controlled by them.

The land within the area subject to inundation is to be converted into a series of ornamental lakes, by dams thrown across the river. It appears to be intended that the stormwater drainage of the town should discharge into the lakes, and if such be the case, they will scarcely be worth the money they will cost to form. In any case no drainage scheme appears to have been considered. Nor does it appear that proper provision has been made for getting rid of flood waters, which will have to get over the crest of the dams upon which roads will have to be constructed. These roads must suffer severe damage.

I like the arrangement of the departmental buildings, with their gardens and lawns, situated in close proximity to the Houses of Parliament, the Capitol, and the Governor-General’s and Prime Minister’s residences. This group will make a compact and picturesque quarter of the city.

The City Hall and administration of affairs site is also well situated, with the Post Office, Mint, Civil and Criminal Courts around it, but just why this choice and central position should be polluted by the presence of the Gaol passes my understanding. What would we say in Sydney, or Melbourne, if the Gaol were situated in Martin Place, or in Collins Street.

The University site is a good one, and stands on a well elevated area, with a good, red, sandy, loamy soil, permitting of good horticultural treatment, but the site chosen for the Hospital I must class as a less favourable one, standing as it does on a low spur only 20 feet above flood level, and right over it.

The site will be very costly to prepare, and the streets, not having been located with a due consideration of the contours, will have, in places, some rather unnecessarily steep gradients, some of which will be as severe as 1 in 10, and 1 in 9 even in the principal avenues, while drainage has received no consideration at all.

No. 18.—Second Prize, £750. Finland. This is indeed a bold conception, and one which may be regarded as outside the range of possibility, and here arises the question of why such designs were awarded prizes at all. There can be little doubt that the Commonwealth Government, in offering prizes, did so with a view to getting the best practical ideas, which would be convertible into realities on the site. But have we not here an architectural design of beauty, no doubt, but quite out of the question when we come to actualities. Just imagine the cutting away of hills and filling up of hollows, 50 to 60 feet in depth, in order to flatten out a city site to suit a plan.

No. 4.—The Third Prize. France. This gives us another instance of a prize being given for artistic reasons principally. The drawings are indeed very beautiful, and the design on paper is very effective, but little consideration has been given to the engineering difficulties. One street, for the purpose of maintaining symmetry, has been taken along a precipitous drop, so that one side of the road is 35 feet lower than the other. This and other defects of a like nature could easily have been avoided, but the designer evidently thought that such considerations should not weigh when balanced on the other side by symmetrical considerations apparent on paper, and on paper only, for they would be quite unappreciable on the ground. However, he was right, for he got the 3rd Prize. As designs on paper, leaving out all considerations of practical utility, I can understand Nos. 1 and 3 appealing to members of the Board who were not surveyors, but I cannot so easily understand how No. 2 got its place.

No. 10.—First Minority Report. Australian. This appears without doubt to have received from its authors the most practical consideration of any plan in the exhibition. It shows evidence of having been studied from every point of view. The treatment does not catch the eye perhaps so much as Nos. 1 and 3, nor even so much as some of the other designs shown. Nevertheless it will make a beautiful city from the aesthetic point of view, while its practicability is far and away above any of its competitors. The design has been prepared to fit the site. The streets, though well chosen as to location, do not suffer from want of symmetry. The public buildings are well arranged, whilst the sewerage and stormwater drains have received proper attention. This design also has some artistic work attached to it in the form of views. In one or two places the form of the allotments is undesirable.

No. 53.—This also, I understand, is an Australian design, and, in my estimation, is one of the gems of the exhibition. It has been entirely overlooked, not even getting honourable mention. Nevertheless, it has received very able treatment and reflects much credit upon its author. The streets are well designed, with due regard to grades, and the town well laid out. The city proper and residential quarters generally, have been kept in the south, and I think the correct side of the river, because of the better protection from winds. It is thereby rendered more compact than many of the others, where half a mile of water is going to intervene between two parts of the city. It is entitled the city of the Southern Cross, and it attracted much attention, being actually mistaken for part of the winning design in one of our dailies. Its treatment is irregular in form.

No. 81.—Third Minority—nationality not known. This is another very effective design which fairly well takes natural features into consideration. It shows an outer circle avenue, is geometrical in centre, then radial and irregular. The river appears to be confined within its banks, and the flooded area utilised no doubt by being filled in above flood level.

No. 47 is a fine plan, well conceived, and which has taken the natural features somewhat into consideration, but the alluvial flats will require much expenditure on them in order to carry out the design. This is the most artistic production of them all, and the picture effects in crayon are superb. Owing, however, to the artistic work, the details have been much obscured, but apparently the rivers and flats have been covered over entirely.

No. 61 is an example of a square design, with its faces 45 degrees from the cardinal points. It has sacrificed everything for the purpose of carrying out a paper ideal. The drawing is a pretty one, and gives one the idea of a competition for a postage stamp. Parliament House occupies the centre of a circle, round which are grouped Government offices, outside these again are more public offices, arranged in the form of a square. The whole of the foregoing are in gardens, and would, without a doubt, give a most charming effect. Having now formed his square, he keeps enlarging it by first the business portion, then by a zone of first-class residences, then second-class residences, then by gardens, then finally, by an outer zone of third-class residences. Expansion beyond this limit being provided for irregularly. The design is unique and worth attention.

No. 54 is also somewhat remarkable. A number of nuclei have been selected, round which streets run. Radial avenues connect these and the intervening spaces are filled in either irregularly or by smaller circular nuclei.

No. 15. England. This is an effective design, but only covers a small portion of the area around the principal features near the centre of the area and south of the river. Natural features have received due consideration. The design is circular, radial, and geometrical.

No. 69 is of no value at all as a design. It is a grotesque and bizarre production, which I heard a bystander liken to a drunkard’s dream, perhaps because of its general likeness to a number of purple entwined snakes.

No. 51 is another singular conception in the form of a lozenge or oval, occupying only a small portion of the central area. Such productions set one wondering at the strange ideas entertained by some as to what the requirements of a capital city would be.

No. 35.—Honourable Mention. Is an example in which semi-circles, or rather arches, inner and outer give the form, with radial avenues and square or oblong treatment internally.

No 42 is a good example of town-planning, embodying square, radial, and sectional treatment, but it is not extensive enough.

No. 2 is a too intensely practical and utilitarian design for the purpose. It has been well considered as to contours, but is not sufficiently aesthetic for a political capital.

No. 60 is another good example of planning, partly irregular and partly geometrical in which features have been attented to.

No. 43.—Australian. Would make a very fine city, but owing to the enormous area of streets, would be costly to maintain, notwithstanding that the features have been studied. The public buildings are on the wrong side of the river.

No. 40 is an extraordinary conception, comprising two principal, circular, and radial systems, with several smaller ones, which reminds the spectator more of an elaborate piece of tatting or mosaic work than anything else. It goes right over everything, and is not practical.

No. 66 is an oblong design, with absolutely straight streets running to the cardinal points, with parks along the river bank. It goes over everything as all such designs must.

No. 140 would make a good chessboard if the number of squares were largely reduced.

No. 100.—Jacob’s coat of many colours is the joke of the exhibition. Drawn up into parallelograms, filled in with vivid colours and pasted over with newspaper clippings, representing motor cars, buildings, parks, industrial areas, &c., &c., it is too funny for anything. I have no doubt its author thought he was having a really good shot at the £1750 at least.

No. 70 is a fairly good representation of an aesthetic city and is well thought out. Its treatment is circular and radial.

No. 41.—2nd Minority Report. This is a fine example of town-planning. The location of roads is good, but the town is cut into too many parts, and some of the best sites are thrown into park land.

No. 5 is a good example of what a city should not be. It is cut up into squares and geometrical figures without relief.

No. 6.—A utilitarian city not suited to requirements.

No. 59 is another good example, with an outer circle and the inner parts somewhat concentric and radial. He fills in the river and its flats, and cuts a canal, utilising all the filled in lands for various purposes.

No. 7.—America. Honourable Mention. Is a good example of the geometrical. The lowland is formed into lakes, while the north portion is designed in the form of a hexagon, divided into sections by concentric hexagons and radial avenues. The southern portion is cut into fanlike sections and squares. Contours not sufficiently considered.

No. 25.—A circular design divided into inner and outer circles, with radial avenues and figure eight. He confines the river and utilises the flats. No attention paid to contours.

No. 34.—England. This is a fine example of the rectangular, and is evidently the work of an engineer. It would make a fine town, but is a bit too severe for its object.

No. 37.—Another beautiful English design, but the flooded area is all designed for building purposes, &c.

No. 9.—Another example of rectangular work, with diagonals. It is of Australian origin. The contours have been generally ignored.

No. 73.—Another curiosity. It gives one the idea of having been conceived in a sausage factory.

No. 139.—A circular and radial horror. It looks as though it was intended for the glass dome of a crystal palace.

Though I have mentioned less than one quarter of the plans sent in, I have pretty well covered the various styles that have been conceived. There are, of course, other designs of merit among them, but when one considers that the whole world has been drawn upon for its wisdom in evolving ideas for a new city, the reason of whose existence is political and official only, one is struck by the few examples which really show an appreciation of the circumstances under which the designers were working. The city is being brought into existence for one object only, viz., to be the Capital City of this Commonwealth. Where Parliament will sit, where the great offices of State will be situated; where the High Court of Justice will be located, and where the whole Commonwealth will look to for its guidance. It will be the residence principally of those who are directing the affairs of the country. As such, it will be visited by people from all parts of the globe, it will be the show city of Australia, and its principal object is the beautiful. Its buildings must be good; its parks and gardens must be good; its streets must be wide and easy, and well chosen. Its allotments for building must be roomy to admit of artistic horticultural treatment, &c., &c., nor need the business portion of the city suffer. It will probably never be a great business or manufacturing centre, and for that reason its shops, &c., need not be oversmall or crowded. There should be little gardens, plantations, and rockeries, with fountains, statuary, and lawns dotted about in suitable places in the business portions as elsewhere. And in the formation and building of this great Southern Capital we have the opportunity before us now of creating the most beautiful and well thought out city in the world. I have frequently, in mentioning the various plans, stated that the contours had not been sufficiently or at all considered. I must explain that generally the features are not in any way severe, and streets can be taken almost anywhere without getting super severe grades, but that is not the question. It is necessary to locate the streets so that they will best serve their purposes as means of communication, so that they will be well drained; so that they will be suited to show off the buildings and parks and gardens, and so that their construction will be as economical as possible. All these considerations are of much more importance than symmetry of design, and this seems to have been the guiding principle in the majority of the plans sent in.

I think that we may well congratulate ourselves that an Australian design has succeded in gaining so high a place in open competition with the whole world, and we may well regret that the verdict of the Board was not a unanimous one. It is unsatisfactory to find that a design like No. 10, which was so superior in the eyes of one of the most representative surveyors in Australia as to merit first prize, and first only, was unable to find even an honourable mention from the other members of the Board. I do not think that professional men generally endorse that judgment.

## Mr. Chas. H. Caswell’s response

Mr. Chas. H. Caswell said:—

Mr. President, Ladies and Gentlemen,—Speaking for the Collaborators who are the authors of Design No. 10, known as “The Australian Design,” I wish to say how deeply honoured we feel at the flattering terms of approval of our work contained in the President’s highly instructive paper on the subject of the “Federal Capital Designs.” We endorse this criticism of Mr. Curtis, whose life-long practice in town-planning and in engineering surveys, constitutes him as one peculiarly qualified to speak. His criticism also goes to vindicate Mr. Coane in his action in the production of the minority report. I think it will be admitted that it requires a great deal of moral courage to adhere so tenaciously to one’s opinion as did Mr. Coane in the production of that report, and though I may be accused of reflective flattery, I venture to predict that history will yet record the truth of Mr. Coane’s judgment, and thus justify his courageous refusal to accept the majority report. Enough has been said by the President, perhaps, to convince most of you that there is doubt as to the practical possibility of applying any one of the prize-winning designs to the contours of Canberra in its entirety, but if there is any one present to-night who would be more fully convinced, an inspection of the contour plan upon which has been laid down the skeleton of the prize-winning design, should be sufficient. It is a remarkable fact that we, the authors of Design No. 10, in our several efforts in its initiation, persevered with almost the exact conception adopted by Mr. Griffin, the author of Design No. 29, but we found that if we adopted a formal method, its rigidity involved a serious sacrifice of grade and other engineering considerations which tended towards inefficiency, and as our policy was to cater for the votes of the several professional adjudicators, we preferred to go on sound lines rather than risk the disapprobation of any-one.

The President, in his remarks, practically justifies our location of the Railway. On the contrary, my friend, Mr. Sulman, in a contributed article to “Building,” criticises this as a serious defect. In explanation, we were guided by the fact, that the crossing of the Molonglo was located by one of the most competent railway surveyors in Australia, and, as it coincided with our views, we made this crossing a governing factor in the location of the line in its entire length. On the other hand, the American design, in its location of the Railway, is committed to a tunnel under one of its chief centres, and emerges from this on to a viaduct 4800 feet long, bridging the waters of its upper lake, which, by the way, provides an average depth of two feet only. As another instance in the conception of the fitness of things between the Australian and American designs, the treatment of Kurragong Hill is a case in point. The author of the latter selects it as his prime central point, upon which is placed the Capitol, and from which radiate several magnificent avenues. We, of the Australian design, selected this spot as suitable for an outlook over the city of the future owing to its precipitous contours and dominatry prominence, suggesting a national monument on its heights, and treating the sourrounding contours as a Park. The only rational means of access we could devise in ascent, was by a spiral road-way on a grade of 1 in 20. Therefore, what the grades of Mr. W. B. Griffin’s magnificent avenues must be, as converging on this spot, I will leave you to conjecture, and Mr. Griffin insists on retaining the contours of this young mountain as the key to his conception.

We regard any attempt to destroy the contour of this prominent feature as little short of vandalism. In forming our syndicate of specialists of modest pretensions, we were convinced that *no one man* could successfully design the Federal City, and we set to work, resolved to a “give and take” policy. Studying the pros and cons, one or other of us often had to suppress a tear, as our cherished conception was ruthlessly brushed aside to make way for the will of the majority. However, we were finally unanimous in the adoption of the design, as you have seen it, and, though many times since it went into the hands of the judges, have we met and discussed as to how we could have improved it. Now, after having been educated by the conceptions of the world’s town-planners, we are unanimous in the conclusion that we have produced our best in Design No. 10, and would submit it again at any time in a similar competition for the best design of the Federal City at Canberra. In conclusion, Mr. President, and Ladies and Gentlemen,—I must say on behalf of my colleagues, that we have been greatly touched by the patriotic appreciation bestowed on our efforts by the public and experts who attended the exhibition of the designs; this has, in a large measure, compensated us for the disappointment—a disappointment free from mercenary motives—that our Australian design had just failed to prove to the world that Australian professionals, as in all classes of sport in the past, were also pre-eminent in the more practical art of town-planning. We still adhere to a fervent trust, however, that the Australian Federal City at Canberra will be created upon an Australian design.

## Subsequent discussion and explanation of Design No. 10

Subsequent to the paper having been read, and to Mr. Caswell’s address, at the request of the meeting, Mr. Caswell then proceeded to give a description of the main features of Design No. 10, and comparative descriptions on points where it differed from the First Prize Design and others.

By means of the model of the contours of the Canberra site, together with the skeleton contoured plan upon which the main avenues of the First Prize Design had been plotted, Mr. Caswell described the conception which formed the basis of that design. The main central point selected was Kurrajong Hill, from which radiated a number of avenues in various directions. These were directed towards prominent features, such as Mount Ainslie, Black Mountain, Shale Hill, Red Hill, Mount Russell, and Mount Vernon. Between many of these secondary features other avenues were directed, the object being to secure interesting and effective closed vistas in the street views. The disregard of the author to efficiency of grade in his conception became easily apparent, as it disclosed that the approach to the “Capitol,” situated upon the summit of Kurrajong, involved grades of 1 in 8 to 1 in 14. The site selected for Government House is on the west side, and adjacent to the “Capitol,” on the steep slopes of the Hill, about 100 feet below the summit. The position of the railway was then shown, also the site of the railway station, which indicated that exceedingly heavy earthworks were involved, succeeded by a viaduct crossing the ornamental water, of unnecessary dimensions.

Design No. 10 was then explained. It was pointed out that the apparent irregularity of the Australian design was due to the fact that it was governed by engineering considerations, and that the wide space between the north and south portions of the city was the result of the necessity to provide that no polluted water should enter the ornamental lake; neglect of this feature, Mr. Caswell said, would prove fatal to any design which embraced ornamental water in the valley of the Molonglo River. The intervening spaces between the lake and stormwater drains were reserved for parks, so as to ensure, as far as possible, the purity of any water which should escape into the lake. Two main channels were provided, one on either side of the lake, intercepting polluted water only from about 1000 acres each. Pure water from the back areas was conducted through the natural channels to the lake. Polluted water was to be discharged into the Molonglo below the site of the dam.

To provide for ornamental water, a dam was proposed to be constructed across the Molonglo, near the western boundary, about 50 feet high. This would back up the water across to the eastern boundary of the city. This produced a lake of about 1000 acres, with a mean depth of 10 feet.

To ensure the safety of the dam, a spillway was provided, about 50 chains north of the dam site, by cutting through a peninsula formed by a sharp bend in the river. This involves heavy earthworks.

In the American design, this feature seems to be entirely overlooked in the design of the various dam sites.

A sewerage system was also provided in Design No. 10, on the basis of information supplied in the book of Requirements of the Competition. A main gravitation sewer was located on the south side of the ornamental water, to intercept the sewerage of the larger section of the southern portion of the city. The low level area, which could not be reached by the gravitation sewer, was divided into three districts, and the sewage was to be collected and pumped into the gravitation sewer. The whole of the north side of the city was treated as low level, and the sewage was arranged to be collected and pumped from the north side into the gravitation sewer on the south side.

The railway line, as located, was slightly deviated from the original position laid down on the contour plan. On the north side this was thought necessary in order to secure an uninterrupted view of the magnificent panoramic landscape from the site of the secondary railway station, and on the south side in order to add to the symmetry of design, and to secure a more advantageous position for the primary railway station. Mr. Caswell said he was inclined to favour the position for the railway line of route adopted by the author of the third design recommended in the Minority Report, providing the foundation for the bridging of the Molonglo was satisfactory.

In answer to a question by one of the audience, Mr. Caswell said the honour of the discovery of the Canberra site was due to Colonel Vernon, and that the position now adopted was the choice of a board of advisers consisting of Colonel Vernon, Colonel Miller, and Mr. Scrivener.
